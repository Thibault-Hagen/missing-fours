The Missing Fours

Companies report earnings per share rounded to the cent. I rebuilt the unrounded number from the SEC filings of thousands of public companies, to look at the one digit that rounding throws away. It should be a 4 one time in ten.

By Thibault Hagen

A tenth of a cent

Two kinds of EPS. Basic EPS divides by shares actually outstanding. Diluted EPS also counts shares that options, convertibles and other securities could create. Diluted is the headline number, so it is the one used here; basic EPS is checked too.

Earnings per share is net income for the common shareholders divided by the average number of shares outstanding. Companies publish it to two decimal places, so a quarter that works out to $0.1249 is reported as $0.12, and one that works out to $0.1250 is reported as $0.13. The gap between those two quarters is a hundredth of a cent. The gap in the headline is a full cent.

That cent matters because analysts publish their consensus estimate to the cent, and a company that misses it by a penny can see its stock fall. The tenth-of-a-cent digit is where a small judgment call (a bonus accrual trimmed, a reserve released a quarter early) can turn a 4 into a 5, and a miss into a match.

Why only EPS of $0.10 or more. In small numbers, low digits are more common than high ones (Benford's law). From $0.10 up, the tenth of a cent is the third significant digit or later, where every digit is within 0.2 points of 10%.

If nobody touched the numbers, that digit would be close to random, with each of 0 through 9 turning up about 10% of the time. A shortage of 4s, with a matching surplus of 5s, is the fingerprint of rounding up on purpose.

Three finance and law researchers, one of them former SEC commissioner Joseph Grundfest, named the pattern quadrophobia. Studying company reports from 1980 to 2017, they found that 4 was the rarest digit in that spot, and that companies avoiding it were more likely to restate their results later and to face SEC enforcement or shareholder lawsuits. The SEC's Division of Enforcement uses data analytics built on the same idea. Its EPS Initiative, which announced its first cases in 2020, has been reported to screen for companies with too few 4s, and its cases include Interface, Fulton Financial, Healthcare Services Group, Rollins and Gentex.

Both of those efforts rely on paid or private data. This site rebuilds the digit from free public SEC filings, for every company whose filings contain the numbers needed, and redoes it every week.

When the result is a loss

Rounding works the other way for a loss. A loss of $0.1249 per share is reported as $(0.12), a loss of $0.1250 as $(0.13). A company trying to look better wants to stay below the line, so if the pattern comes from deliberate rounding, losses should show extra 4s, not missing ones.

Who rounds

The SEC sorts companies by size into filer categories, using the market value of shares held by outside investors. Large accelerated filers have $700 million or more and draw the most analyst attention, so if the pressure comes from analysts' estimates, they might be expected to round the most.

    How this works

    Every number on this site comes from the XBRL data companies file with their 10-Qs and 10-Ks. For each company and quarter, a script takes the first filing that reported the quarter, divides net income available to common shareholders by the weighted-average diluted share count, checks that the result rounds back to the EPS the company printed, and reads the digit after the cent. The inputs are themselves rounded, usually to the thousand, so a digit is used only when every value those inputs allow gives the same digit. A new run happens every Saturday.
