The Missing 4s

How it works

Every number on this site can be traced back to a filing on SEC EDGAR. This page explains each step, the checks that throw data out, and what the results can and cannot show.

The steps

  1. Pull every company's XBRL data

    Since 2009, companies have tagged the numbers in their 10-Q and 10-K filings in XBRL, a format computers can read. The SEC publishes all of them every night in one archive, companyfacts.zip. Each Saturday a GitHub Actions job downloads it and keeps nine facts per period: reported basic and diluted EPS, the EPS numerators, net income, and the weighted-average share counts. It keeps only single quarters and full years.

  2. Keep the version first reported

    Every 10-K repeats the prior year's numbers, sometimes restated or adjusted for a stock split. The question here is about what a company published at the time, so for each period the script keeps the numbers from the first filing that reported it as its own current period, and ignores every later repeat.

  3. Recalculate EPS

    Audit procedure: recalculation.

    Diluted EPS = net income available to common shareholders ÷ weighted-average diluted shares outstanding

    If a company did not tag the "available to common shareholders" figure, its net income is tried instead. Basic EPS is rebuilt the same way, with basic shares, as a cross-check.

  4. Tie it back to the reported number

    Audit procedure: agree to the source document.

    The recalculated EPS has to round to the EPS the company printed. When it doesn't, the quarter is dropped rather than guessed at. The usual reason is the two-class method, where companies subtract earnings set aside for participating securities before dividing.

  5. Account for rounding in the inputs

    Filings usually report in thousands, so net income of $12,437 thousand could be anything from $12,436,500 to $12,437,500, and the share count has the same issue. The script works out the lowest and highest EPS those ranges allow, and uses the digit only when both ends agree on it. All of the arithmetic uses exact fractions, so floating-point error can never move a value across a rounding line.

  6. Read the digit

    The digit tested is the third decimal place of the unrounded EPS: the tenth of a cent. Only quarters with EPS of at least $0.10, profit or loss, are used. Below $0.10 that position is close to the first significant digit, where Benford's law makes low digits more common even in honest data.

  7. Compare with chance

    Audit procedure: analytical procedure, comparing recorded amounts with an expectation.

    If nothing nudged the numbers, each digit should appear about 10% of the time. The tests are described below.

How much data survives each check

Counts of company-quarters at the latest rebuild. Full years go through the same steps and are reported separately.

The statistics

All companies together

The digit counts are compared with a model in which every digit has probability 0.10, using a chi-square goodness-of-fit test with 9 degrees of freedom.

χ² = Σ (observed − expected)² / expected

Every expected count is in the thousands, far above the usual minimum of 5. These quarters are not a random sample from a larger population; they are all of the reports. What the test asks is whether the process that produced the digits behaves like a fair ten-sided die.

The share of 4s on its own gets a one-proportion z-test against 0.10 and a 95% confidence interval, p̂ ± 1.96√(p̂(1 − p̂)/n). The before-and-after comparison around the SEC's first EPS Initiative cases uses a two-proportion z-test.

One company at a time

For a single company with n tested quarters, the number of 4s is modeled as binomial with n trials and probability 0.10, and so is the number of 5s. Each company gets two one-sided tests: the probability of seeing that few 4s or fewer, and the probability of seeing that many 5s or more. They are only reported once a company has 20 tested quarters, because with fewer the tests have almost no power: even a company that never reported a 4 in 10 quarters would have a 35% chance of that by luck.

Running two tests on more than a thousand companies guarantees some false alarms. At a 1% cutoff, about 1 test in 100 would be flagged even if no company rounded on purpose. The false-alarm rate shown for each company is a Benjamini–Hochberg q-value computed across every test: the estimated share of companies flagged at that level that would be flagged by luck alone.

Checks on the method

A placebo digit. The same pipeline reads the next digit too, the hundredth of a cent. Nobody gains anything by managing that digit, so it should come out flat, and it does (see the table on the story page). If the method itself created patterns, they would show up here.

Tie-out failures. The tie-out to reported EPS throws out quarters whose rebuilt EPS rounds to a different cent. That is not neutral. For companies whose rebuilt EPS runs slightly high (usually because of two-class or other adjustments the XBRL tags don't capture), the quarters that fail are the ones just below the half-cent line, and their place in the data is left empty just above it, which removes 5s. Runs slightly low remove 4s instead. Most one-cent failures run high, so this effect works against finding extra 5s.

Clean reconstructions. To avoid that problem, the market-wide test is repeated using only companies whose every testable quarter ties out (at least 8 of them). For these companies the reconstruction is very likely exact. The shortage of 4s is larger in this group and the surplus of 5s appears, and the half-cent chart on the story page uses this group.

Other versions. The test is also run on basic EPS, and on only those quarters where the company tagged its exact EPS numerator. All of the versions are listed on the story page.

What this can't tell you

A shortage of 4s at one company is not evidence of wrongdoing. Accruals and estimates involve judgment every quarter, and a company can reach the next cent through real decisions, like timing a shipment, that are legal and properly recorded. The pattern is strongest as a statement about companies in aggregate.

Large companies are under-represented. Many report net income in millions and shares in millions, which leaves too much rounding to pin down the digit, so their quarters are set aside as too imprecise rather than guessed at.

Analysts' consensus usually targets adjusted, non-GAAP EPS, which is not in XBRL. This site tests GAAP diluted EPS, the number in the filing itself.

XBRL has tagging errors, and companies change tags over time. The tie-out to reported EPS removes most bad inputs, but a quarter can occasionally pass with a numerator that is slightly off, which pushes its digit toward random and makes the results here, if anything, conservative.

Filer category is each company's current status, not its status in every past quarter.

About

The Missing Fours was built by Thibault Hagen. The code that downloads, checks and tests the data is open source on GitHub, and the whole pipeline runs on free GitHub services. If a number looks wrong, open an issue there with the company and the quarter.

Built by Thibault Hagen. Data from SEC EDGAR XBRL filings, rebuilt every week.
